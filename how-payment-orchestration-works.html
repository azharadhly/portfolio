How payment orchestration works

A business that takes payments in many countries ends up with many payment providers, each with its own API, fees and approval rates. An orchestration layer such as Fincashier sits in front of all of them: the business integrates once, and the platform decides where each payment goes.

01Why orchestrate

No single payment provider is best everywhere. One approves more cards in Germany, another is cheaper in the US, a third handles crypto, and any of them can go down. Connecting to each one separately means a new integration, a new dashboard and new reconciliation every time.

A payment orchestration platform puts one API in front of all of them. Fincashier describes itself as exactly that: the business integrates once and Fincashier handles the providers behind it, more than 80 of them according to its site.

02Creating a payment

Everything starts with a payment session. The merchant sends the amount in minor units (2500 means €25.00), the currency, the customer and how the payment should be routed:

POST /v1/sessions

{
  "amount": 2500,
  "currency": "EUR",
  "customer": { "id": "cus_8841" },
  "routing": {
    "strategy": "performance",
    "retries": 3,
    "failover": true
  }
}

The response carries the session id, a status of pending, approved or failed, and the provider that took the payment. Requests accept an idempotency key, so a retried call never charges twice.

03Smart routing

The router picks a provider for each payment using live numbers. The site says approval rates, latency and error rates update every 60 seconds. The routing strategy decides what best means:

  • performance: the highest approval rate.
  • cost: the lowest fee.
  • geo: the provider that works best for the customer’s country.
  • round_robin: take turns, to spread the volume.
  • custom: the merchant’s own rules.

04Failover cascades

If the first provider declines or doesn’t answer, the payment can move to the next one in an ordered list, such as Stripe, then Adyen, then Checkout.com. The customer only sees one payment that went through.

Not every decline should be retriedA soft decline, such as a timeout or a bank that’s briefly unavailable, is worth trying elsewhere. A hard decline, such as a card reported stolen, should stop the cascade. Retrying it makes a business look risky to the card networks.

05Webhooks and reconciliation

Every change is reported by webhook, with events such as session.approved, session.rerouted and refund.processed. Each is signed with HMAC-SHA256 and retried with exponential backoff, and the site says events stay in a log for 72 hours so they can be replayed. Settlements from every provider are then matched against the transactions automatically, the same reconciliation shown in Note 02.

06More than taking payments

The same layer sends payouts to banks, cards and wallets, one at a time or in batches. It keeps every provider’s API keys in one vault with role-based access, runs fraud rules before a payment is routed, and offers a hosted or embedded checkout that decides when to ask for 3-D Secure.

07Words to know

Payment orchestration
One layer that connects to many payment providers and routes between them.
PSP
Payment service provider, such as Stripe or Adyen.
Routing strategy
The rule that decides which provider gets a payment.
Cascade
An ordered list of providers to try when one fails.
Soft decline
A temporary failure that may succeed on retry or elsewhere.
Approval rate
The share of payment attempts a provider approves.
Minor units
Amounts as whole cents, so 2500 means 25.00.
Idempotency key
A unique key that makes a repeated request safe.
